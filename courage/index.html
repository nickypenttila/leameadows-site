The Cost of Courage

The sister no one was watching finds the courage to tell what she knows — and pays for it.

Lydia had shown her the letters because Lydia could not bear to have a wonderful thing and no one to envy her for it.

That was the part Kitty understood and could never afterward make anyone else understand: she had not gone looking. She had been lying on her own bed with her face to the wall, wishing to be asleep, when Lydia came in with a candle and a folded paper, sat down hard enough to tip Kitty toward her, and said, You will die, Kitty, only look what he has written, in the bright greedy whisper she used for secrets.

So Kitty had read.

The letters came through Harriet Forster. Mr. Wickham wrote to Lydia inside Harriet's correspondence, his words tucked among a colonel's wife's chatter like a knife wrapped in a handkerchief. Particular affection. He had written that. He had written that Brighton would want only Lydia to be complete, and Lydia had read it aloud with her eyes shining as though she had been handed the world.

"He loves me," she had said. "Mr. Wickham loves me, and Mary King may keep her ten thousand pounds and her freckles. You are only jealous. It is because no one has ever written you a letter and no one ever will, because you have no more conversation than a hen, and if you breathe one word of this I shall never tell you anything again as long as I live."

No one has ever written you a letter and no one ever will.

The terrible thing was not that it was cruel. Lydia said cruel things as easily as she kicked off a shoe, and generally with as little notion of where they might land. The terrible thing was that, as far as Kitty could see, it was true. She had spent her whole life half a step behind a younger sister who burned brighter, telling herself that standing in Lydia's warmth was nearly as good as having a fire of one's own.

And now Lydia's fire was going to Brighton, where a man who wrote knives into handkerchiefs would be waiting, and Kitty was the only one who knew.

For four nights she had lain awake with the secret under her ribs, hot and ugly and impossible to put down. It was like swallowing a coal: the terror of not knowing whether it was worse to keep it in or let it burn its way out. If she said nothing, Lydia went to Brighton and Kitty kept her sister. If she spoke — if she carried the coal downstairs and set it, burning, before her father — Lydia would not go, and Lydia would hate her. The single breath that had held them together all her life, KittyandLydia, would be cut clean in two; Kitty would be, for the first time, only Kitty.

She was not brave. Brave people, Kitty thought, probably knew where to put their hands and did not lie staring at the bedpost wishing the bedpost could go downstairs instead. She wanted someone else to have known, so that someone else could be the one to ruin everything.

The window greyed toward morning. A blackbird started up in the hawthorn by the gate, careless and liquid, singing as though there were nothing in the world but the morning.

Kitty got up, washed her face in the cold water, and went down to break the thing she could not carry any longer. Somewhere in the long grey hours, she had discovered the one fact that decided it: she would rather lose Lydia than be the sister who had known.

She could not eat. Lydia was not at table yet, and the empty chair beside Kitty was the only mercy the morning had offered. If Kitty spoke now, Lydia would not be there to stop her. If she waited, Lydia might come in laughing, glowing, terrible, and Kitty knew she would never say it then.

She sat in her place with a piece of toast and reduced it, methodically, to a heap of crumbs smaller and smaller, because her hands had to do something while the words would not come. Her heart went faster and faster, a kettle with the lid beginning to lift. Across the table Lizzy looked tired and far away, the way she had looked since she came back from Kent, turned in upon some trouble of her own. Jane sat beside her, quiet and pale over an untouched cup of tea.

At Kitty's left, Mary had the drawn look that meant she had a sermon loaded and was waiting for the pause to fire it. Mama talked. Mama always talked. The sound of it went over Kitty like water, and underneath the water the coal burned, and she thought: now. Say it now. Before Mama draws breath. If you wait for a good moment there will never be a good moment, there has never once in your life been a moment that was yours.

She had meant to say it carefully. As she lay awake, she'd chosen the words so many times, arranging them, making them reasonable and calm so that her father would see she was not being spiteful, not being a child, not being Kitty. She had a whole sensible speech.

What came out, when it came, came out sideways and too fast, ahead of the speech, ahead of her courage, ahead of everything:

"Mama, Lydia has been writing to Mr. Wickham."

And then it was said, and could not be unsaid, and the kettle had boiled over, and Kitty sat in the wreck of her own toast with her heart slamming and waited for the world to understand what she had done.

The world did not understand. That was the first cruelty, the one she had not braced for. She had imagined gravity, alarm, her father's face going still. She had not imagined that her mother would clasp her hands together and arrive, by some appalling private road, at delight.

"In love!" Mama cried, when Kitty had stammered out a little more — the affection, the secret letters, Brighton. "In love with Mr. Wickham! Well, I cannot say I am astonished, he is so very handsome, and Lydia has such a way — Kitty, why did you not say so before?"

Why did you not say so before.

Kitty felt the question land on her like a slap, because she could not answer it. She had said it before to no one because saying it had cost four nights and the whole of her courage and very probably her sister, and Mama had turned it, in a breath, into a piece of happy gossip Kitty had been too dull to share sooner. The coal she had carried down the stairs with both hands, terrified, was being received as though she had brought in the post.

She looked at her father.

He had not said anything. He had been looking at her — really looking, in a way that almost never happened, because in a house of five daughters Kitty was the fourth, the spare, the echo, and her father's attention, when it came at all, came as a joke aimed somewhere past her head. He was not joking now. His face had gone bare of all its usual wit, stripped down to something underneath that she had no name for and had certainly never seen turned on herself.

He took off his spectacles. He folded them, slowly, and set them on the table beside his plate with a deliberateness that stopped even Mama's mouth for the space of three whole seconds.

"Kitty," he said. "Tell me what you know."

Her hands were twisting the napkin in her lap, twisting and twisting. She did not look at her mother. She looked at her father, and whatever was in her face was beyond hiding now: not triumph, not spite, only a plea. Please listen. Please believe me. Please let me be right about one thing.

"They write to each other," she said. "Only his letters come inside Mrs. Forster's, so no one knows they are from him. Lydia showed me. She would not stop showing me. She says he has — that he has a particular affection for her, and that when the regiment goes to Brighton —" The words ran out from under her. She swallowed and made herself find more. "She says I am jealous and stupid. She says I do not understand what it is to be admired."

The last of it came out fast and uneven, and the moment it was out she wanted it back, because it was true and it was naked and she had not meant to give it to a whole breakfast table. Jealous and stupid. She had handed them the very stick Lydia would beat her with, and handed it over in her own voice.

The room was silent. Even Mama was silent.

Her father looked at her a moment longer. Then he stood. And at the door — he had risen and crossed half the room before she understood he meant to act at all — he turned back, and said the thing that broke her open.

"Kitty. You will come to me in the library in one quarter of an hour." A pause. And then, not as a joke, not over the top of his spectacles, but plainly, to her, as though she were a person: "You have done right to speak."

You have done right to speak.

Kitty's face did something it had never done before in company, something she could not stop and could not govern: it crumpled — not into tears, there were no tears yet, but into pure relief, a relief so vast it had no composure anywhere to contain it. She nodded once, hard, and looked down at the ruin of her toast so that no one would see what four words from her father had done to her.

Beside her — she felt it before she saw it — Jane's hand came over her own. Steady. Warm. Saying nothing, because nothing needed saying. The same hand Jane gave to everyone in trouble, and it had never before been given to Kitty, because Kitty was never the one in trouble; Kitty was only ever in the background of someone else's.

She held on to it and did not let herself cry, because the worst of the morning had not even begun, and Lydia's empty chair was still there beside her, waiting.

Mary said something about secrets shared too late. Kitty barely heard it. She heard the door instead.

Lydia swept in with her bonnet already on, bright with news, and for one dreadful second Kitty saw her as everyone else always saw her: all color and motion, all certainty, the room bending toward her before she had crossed it. Then Lydia stopped. Her eyes went from Mama's handkerchief to Papa's empty chair to Kitty's ruined toast, and the brightness went sharp.

"What has happened?" Lydia said. "What has Kitty done?"

Kitty could not stay. She got up so quickly her chair struck the wainscot, and no one stopped her.

The library was the room in the house Kitty had entered least in all her life. It was her father's country, and her father's country had never had much use for her.

He shut the door. He did not sit behind the desk and make her stand in front of it, which she had braced for. He put her in the chair by the cold hearth and took the other one himself, and looked at her, and for a long moment said nothing at all, and Kitty discovered that being looked at by her father with full attention was a thing she did not know how to bear. She had wanted it her whole life. Now that she had it she wanted to crawl under the chair.

"Tell me the whole of it," he said. "From the beginning. Leave nothing out because you think it makes you look foolish. I am not interested, this morning, in how anyone looks."

So she told him. The candle, the three nights, the letter inside Mrs. Forster's letter, particular affection, Brighton, the bright whisper. She told him she had not gone looking. She told him Lydia had said — and here Kitty could hardly get the words out — that no one would ever write to her, and that Kitty had believed her. She told him she had not known what to do. She told him she had not wanted to tell. She had lain awake for four nights wishing it had been anyone but her. She had betrayed her sister, and her sister would never forgive her, and she had done it anyway because she could not be the sister who had known.

Her father listened. He did not interrupt. He did not make light of it. When she had finished, and was sitting with her wet face in her hands, he was quiet for a while, and then he said something she would keep for the rest of her life, in the place where a person keeps the few sentences that have rearranged them.

"You have done a hard thing," he said, "and done it when silence would have been easier for you. That is not a small matter." He took off his spectacles again, and pressed the bridge of his nose, and for a moment looked very tired and very old. "I do not know yet what all this means. I know enough to know that Brighton is out of the question. I know enough to know that you were right to come to me." He looked at her then, directly, without the shelter of a jest. "And I know enough to say this: whatever noise you hear from anyone in this house today, you are not to let it persuade you that you have behaved foolishly. You have not."

Kitty had come into the library expecting to be scolded, or thanked, or dismissed. She had not expected to be taken seriously — to have a man who had looked past her for seventeen years stop, and turn, and look directly at the thing she had done as if it had weight. It undid her far more thoroughly than any scolding could have. She cried in earnest then, in the cold quiet library. Her father looked, for one helpless moment, like a man who had discovered a daughter in his library and did not know where she had come from. Then he took out his handkerchief and gave it to her.

"There," he said, which was not much, but was not nothing.

He went to his desk after that, and she was grateful for it. It was easier to cry when he was not watching her.

When she came out, a quarter of an hour later, with her eyes red and her chin steadier than it had ever been, Lydia was waiting.

Not waiting. Lydia never waited. Lydia came at her like weather.

Kitty had meant to go to the stairs, to her room, to her bed, where perhaps a morning could be started over if one hid one's face hard enough. She had only got as far as the morning-room door, and then Lydia was upon her.

"You."

Kitty stepped backward through the doorway until the curtain was at her shoulders. She had not meant to hide there. Her body had simply found the nearest thing that looked like not being seen.

"You jealous, spiteful, stupid little — you have ruined it, you have ruined everything, because you cannot bear that anyone should ever look at me and not at you, because no one will ever look at you —" Lydia's voice climbed and broke and climbed again, and every word was a stone, and every stone found its mark, because Lydia had a lifetime of knowing exactly where Kitty was softest. "Harriet is expecting me. The regiment will think me a nobody. I shall be the only girl in all of England kept from the seaside, and by my own sister, my own sister who pretended to be my friend —"

Kitty had been brave once that morning. She had spent the entire supply, every coin of it, in a breakfast room and a library, and there was none left. Under Lydia's fury she did the thing she had always done, the old thing, the reliable thing: she burst into tears — how could there still be more of them? — and she pressed herself into the curtain and wished the cloth would open and swallow her whole.

That, at least, Lydia understood. Tears were familiar territory; tears were proof of victory. She gave a sharp little sound — not quite a laugh, not quite disgust — and swept out again, leaving the door open behind her.

But here was the difference, and Kitty felt it even through the weeping, small and strange and new: she had still done it. The tears did not unsay the words. Lydia would not go to Brighton. Whatever Kitty was — jealous, stupid, a nobody, a hen with no conversation — she had also, this morning, been the one who stopped it. Both things were true at once.

She had not known, before today, that a person could be a coward in a curtain and brave in the same morning, that the two could live in one body and neither cancel the other out.

It was Mary, of all people, who came to her. Kitty had not heard her enter, but there she was, setting aside whatever sermon she had been composing on the wages of imprudence or the duty owed by daughters, and crossing the room with the awkwardness of a person who had never once known what to do with another person's grief and had decided, for the first time, to try anyway.

She did not embrace her. Mary did not embrace. She stood close, shoulder nearly to shoulder, and after a moment she said, stiffly, in the voice she used for quotations: "It is a hard thing to be the only one in a house who has done right. I have often found it so." And then, less stiffly, not a quotation at all, in a small flat voice that was entirely her own: "You were very brave, Kitty. I do not think anyone is going to say so today, with all the noise. So I am saying it now."

Kitty looked at her plain, serious, unbeautiful sister, and felt something turn over in her chest that was not relief and not grief but something she had no practice at — the sense of having been joined, quietly, by someone who had nothing to gain by it.

"Thank you, Mary," she said.

"Yes," said Mary, and patted her once on the arm, hard, the way one might pat a horse one was not sure of, and went back to her chair, her duty discharged, her composure resumed, having performed perhaps the single most graceful act of her life and clearly intending never to refer to it again.

Three days later, the regiment marched out of Meryton. Kitty went down with her sisters to watch the redcoats go.

She had thought she would mind. For weeks the leaving of the regiment had been, in Lydia's telling, the end of all joy, the descent of the world into dreary dullness, and Kitty had echoed it, because echoing Lydia was what she did. Standing now on the high street in the hard April sunshine, watching the column form and the dust come up gold under the horses, she searched herself for the grief she was supposed to feel, and found, a little to her surprise, that it was not there. The redcoats were only redcoats. One of them belonged to a man who had made even a lady's letter unsafe, and she was glad, fiercely and privately glad, that he was marching away from her sister and not toward her with Lydia on his arm.

Lydia, on Elizabeth's other side, did not speak to her. Lydia had not spoken to her in three days, except to say cutting things at the edges of rooms. Perhaps she would not speak to Kitty properly for a long while, and that was a grief — a real one, the realest of the week — because for seventeen years Lydia had been the warm fire Kitty stood beside, and the fire had turned its back. Kitty did not pretend to herself that this was nothing. It was a great deal. It was everything she had been afraid of.

But.

She walked home half a step behind her sisters, as she had walked her whole life, and the lane was loud with hedgerow birds. A wren went off in the hawthorn beside her in a sudden furious rattle of song far too large for so small a bird — all that noise out of a thing that fitted in a child's hand, singing as though it had every right to be heard. Kitty stopped a moment to look at it. Jane and Lizzy walked on, talking, not noticing she had fallen back; Mary, half a step behind her now, stopped too, and looked at the wren, and then at Kitty, and said nothing, and waited.

And Kitty thought — not in fine words, because fine words belonged to Mary, and generally came with a headache attached, but plainly, in a shape she could hold — I did a right thing, and it cost me, and I did it anyway. That is mine. No one can take that from me, not Lydia, not Mama, not anyone. Whatever else I am, I am the girl who spoke.

It was a small thing, a wren's worth of a thing, set against everything she had lost that week.

But it was hers, and it was the first thing that ever had been; and she found, walking home in the loud bright lane, that she was not, after all, only half a step behind. She was simply walking. In her own shoes. Toward her own house. Beginning, very quietly and without any audience at all, to be her own.
