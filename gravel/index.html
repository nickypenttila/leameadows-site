The Gravel Sweep

Mr. Darcy’s side of the meeting at Pemberley — in his own voice.

He had told himself he was riding ahead for the estate.

It was not a lie, precisely. A man who has been five months from his own land does not want for reasons to return to it; there were fences down along the Stony Close boundary, a question about the lower meadows that Hardwick had raised twice in letters and once, plaintively, in a postscript, and a stable wall that had been waiting on his decision since Lady Day.

He had recited all of this to Georgiana across his desk in town, in the even voice of a man arranging his affairs, and she had listened with her new and disconcerting attention, and had said, The Derbyshire air has always done you good, which was not agreement. It was information. His sister had taken, this summer, to handing him his own evasions back across the table, gently, so that he might look at them.

The truth was simpler and less creditable, and he knew it by the second hour on the road. The truth was that he could not have borne three days in a carriage with Caroline Bingley's voice laid over the top of his own thoughts like a hand pressed flat to a wound. He had wanted the road. He had wanted the blessed peace of being on a horse occupied with nothing but weather and distance, the kind of peace in which a man might, if he were disciplined, decline to think.

He had not been disciplined. He had thought of Kent the whole way. He thought of it now, on the last stretch of the south drive, with Pemberley's own air coming up green and warm to meet him. The parlor at Hunsford, the candle, the words he could repeat to himself without a single error of memory because he had repeated them so many times the edges had worn smooth.

Had you behaved in a more gentlemanlike manner. He had spent three months learning the sentence the way a penitent learns a psalm — not to be absolved by it, but because it was true, and he had found, to his own surprise, that he would rather carry a true thing that wounded than a false one that comforted.

So. The estate. He had ridden ahead for the estate.

The bay was tired under him, and he let it walk. They had come fast over the dry roads since the last change, faster than the heat warranted, and he had stripped the right glove off somewhere past Lambton without quite noticing he had done it — the leather sticking, the day too hot for armor. He should have stopped to let the horse cool a mile back. He had not wanted to stop. He had wanted to be home, to be inside his own pale with the door shut behind him and the long July evening to fill with steward's letters that asked questions a man could answer.

The road changed before the house appeared. It always did. The last hot mile gave way to the cooler run of the south drive, where the beeches held yesterday's damp in their roots and the smell of dust thinned under leaf mold, cut grass, and water somewhere below the slope. The bay knew it too; one ear came forward, and its stride, tired as it was, lengthened by habit toward the stable yard and oats and shade.

The drive bent. The trees fell back. The sweep opened before him, pale gravel shimmering in the noon. Beyond it the house stood above the terrace, all long windows and steady shadow, with the river-light moving faintly at the lower edge of the lawn — the way it had stood over every homecoming of his life —

and there was a chaise drawn up at his own door, and a woman beside it with her hand on the carriage door, turning her head.

He knew her before he had time to recognize her.

That was the thing he would try, afterward and for many nights, to account for and could not. There was no instant in which he saw a lady by a carriage and then, upon inspection, found her to be Elizabeth Bennet. The recognition arrived whole and ahead of his will, the way one's own name arrives across a crowded room — not perceived but suffered.

The bay felt the change in him before he knew he had made it; his hand had checked on the rein, and the horse, obedient, dropped from its walk to a halt four yards short of her, and blew out, hugely, into the high still silence.

He heard himself say, "Miss Bennet."

He had not chosen to say it. It was simply that her name had been so long in his mouth, said silently across so many rooms, that the sight of her drew it out of him before his judgment could lay a hand on it. A more guarded man would have waited. He had believed himself a guarded man. He discovered, in the space of one breath on his own gravel, that there were limits to his guard, and that he had just ridden straight past the last of them.

"Mr. Darcy." Her voice came level. He could not tell what it cost her. He could tell nothing. That was the cruelty of it — that he, who had studied her face since Hertfordshire until he could have drawn it with his eyes shut, could read in it now only that she was looking at him, and not by one flicker whether the sight of him pained her.

He swung down. There was nothing else to do; a man does not conduct an acquaintance from horseback in his own avenue, and besides, he could not have stayed in the saddle a moment longer with the blood gone so strangely from his head. His boots met the gravel. He took two steps and stopped, and there he stood, in the full sun, a grown man with ten thousand a year and a name that had stood in this valley for two hundred years, and he had not the smallest idea what to do with his own hands, his own face, or the next four seconds of his life.

She was real. That was the trouble. He had carried an Elizabeth Bennet about with him for three months who was made of memory and reproach, and she had been, in her way, manageable; one can hold a position against a memory. This one had dust on the hem of her traveling-gown and a flush along her cheekbone from the heat and a strand of hair come loose at her temple, and she was looking at him out of a real face with real fatigue under the eyes, and against her he had no position at all.

There were other people. He registered them late, and with something close to shame at how late. A gentleman come up beside the carriage door, square-built and easy, watching with the suspended courtesy of a man who has not been introduced and is too well-bred to presume; a lady within, leaning a little out under her bonnet, her face kind and quick. Her relations. She had said something — he caught the end of it — my uncle and aunt, Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner, of Gracechurch Street.

Gracechurch Street.

He knew, with perfect clarity, what the man he had been last October would have done with Gracechurch Street. That man would have heard the warehouse, the counting-house, the unfashionable end of London; would have cooled by half a degree and believed himself courteous while doing it. He had performed exactly that arithmetic in a Hertfordshire ballroom, on this lady's relations, in this lady's hearing, and had been proud of his own forbearance.

He bowed to Mr. Gardiner of Gracechurch Street as he would have bowed to an earl.

He did it deliberately. In the middle of his own confusion, with his pulse loud in his ears and his wits scattered across the sweep like dropped tackle, one clear corner of his mind knew what was being asked of him, and answered it. Here is the thing she accused you of. Here is the man she described. Be otherwise. Not to win her — he had forfeited the right to try — but because she had been right.

He asked Mr. Gardiner, in a voice he was distantly glad to find steady, whether they had come up that morning from Lambton; he hoped the road had not been too dusty in the heat.

It was the smallest civility a gentleman owed a gentleman. He offered it as though it were nothing, and only he knew that every word of it had been chosen against the man he had once been.

Mr. Gardiner returned that the drive had been very pleasant, and the morning a great pleasure, and the housekeeper had shown them the house with admirable patience.

So they had been through the house. Through his rooms, his gallery, the places where his life stood open in books left face-down and chairs drawn near windows and portraits that had no courtesy to soften what they showed. Elizabeth Bennet had walked through Pemberley while he was still persuading himself he had come home to be alone.

"Mrs. Reynolds is patience itself," Darcy said. "She has had practice." And his mouth moved at the corner before he could prevent it — the expression he disliked in his own portrait, the one that was not for company — because there was, even now, even here, something in the picture of Mrs. Reynolds conducting Elizabeth Bennet gravely through his rooms that was very nearly funny, if one had the stomach to find anything funny while the ground was tilting.

The uncle smiled at it. The aunt smiled at it. Elizabeth did not smile. Elizabeth said nothing at all, and stood there in the sun, and he understood — slowly, the way one understands a thing one would rather not — that she was as unstrung by this as he was. He had been so wholly occupied with the wreck of his own composure that it had not, until that moment, occurred to him that hers might be in any danger.

The discovery did him no good whatever. It only made him want, with an intensity that frightened him, to say something — anything — that would let her set her feet under her again; and he had nothing to give her but more weather, more road, more of the small dead coin of polite inquiry.

A boy ran up for the bay. Darcy gave over the rein without looking at it. He asked how long they meant to remain in the country, and heard, through her uncle's answer, that they were at Lambton four days more. He bowed. He asked after Mr. Bennet's family — directed the question to Mr. Gardiner, by the form of it, and aimed it, by the turn of his shoulder, at her, and knew that she knew he had.

"They were all well," she said, "when I last had a letter."

"I am glad."

And then a silence, of the kind that in any drawing-room of the previous winter she would have filled effortlessly, with some light turn that made the whole company easier. Here, she did not fill it. She stood and let it lie there between them on the gravel.

He had used to find her readiness almost a provocation — the quickness, the way she was never at a loss. He found her silence now almost unbearable, because he could read in it, at last, one true thing: that she was not at her ease with him. That whatever else had changed or not changed, she could no longer perform him the small mercy of pretending this was an ordinary meeting.

Her uncle — and Darcy could have gone down on the gravel and thanked the man — turned then and said something easy to his wife about the heat, and stepped across to settle her parasol where it would not catch, and gave them, by that one act of plain good sense, ten seconds of the sweep to themselves.

Darcy took half a step. Not toward her. Toward the carriage door, which was a thing a man might be understood to approach for a reason. He lowered his voice.

"I had not known," he said, "you were to be in Derbyshire."

He looked, while he said it, at the lawn beyond the carriage, because he could not say it to her face. And as it left him he heard everything that was in it, and was powerless to call any of it back.

On the surface it was an apology of form — I would have warned the household, I would have given orders that the family was from home, I would have spared you the awkwardness of meeting me on my own gravel. He meant that. Underneath it, plain to read: I came home believing I would not see you. I came north, in part, because I believed I would not see you. I arranged three months of my life around the certainty of not seeing you, and here you stand at my door.

He had not decided, in the half-second before he spoke, which sentence he was making. He had said the one sentence that did the work of both, and left it to her, and that was either the most honest thing he had ever done or the most cowardly, and he did not know which.

"My uncle had a fancy to see this part of the country," she said. "He had not been so far north before."

"It is fine country."

"Yes."

He let himself look at her then — at her face, directly, for the first time since the bow. He permitted himself perhaps three-quarters of a second of it before he sent his eyes away again to the safety of the carriage, because longer than that and he did not trust what his face would do.

"You are at Lambton?"

"At the Rose."

"I—" he began.

He had been going to say something. He was never afterward able to reconstruct what; it had not got far enough toward existence to leave a trace. I should be glad — I hope you will permit — my sister returns on Monday and would — Some opening, some small reckless attempt to set a thread across the gulf before the carriage carried her off and the chance closed.

He felt it form and felt his judgment fall on it like a portcullis. He had no right. She had told him in a parlor in Kent exactly what his attentions were worth to her, and a man who has been told that and presses on regardless is not ardent; he is no gentleman. To press himself upon her now, on his own ground, where she could not in courtesy refuse to stand and hear him — that was the very offence she had named. He would not commit it twice.

"I hope," he said instead, "you have not found the morning too warm."

"Not at all."

"I am glad."

He had kept them standing long enough. They were leaving; the carriage was waiting; every civility now required him to let them go. He turned a degree toward her, bowed, and held out his hand to help her into the carriage.

She took it.

He had forgotten the glove. He had stripped it off miles back, on the hot road, and had not once thought of it since — not through the recognition, not through the bow, not through any of the ruin of the past five minutes — until her hand came down into his bare one, her own gloved, the kid cool and smooth over the warmth of her, and the whole of his attention contracted to the single square inch of his palm. He had handed her into a carriage bare-handed, as if in a fever, forgetting every rule of the order he had been bred to.

A small impropriety. No one would remark it. He should have regretted it.

He found he would not have given it back for the estate entire.

She stepped up. Her aunt's hand was at her elbow. She did not look at him. She found her seat on the river side, in the shade of the hood, and put her gloved hands in her lap and turned her face to the window with all the composure she possessed, and he stood on his own gravel and watched the steps put up and the door shut.

The chaise drew off. He did not move. He told himself he would go in directly, that to stand watching a departing carriage like a stable-boy was beneath him, that there was the bay to see to and Hardwick to find and a hundred answerable questions waiting in the estate office. He did not move.

He watched as the trees of the drive closed around it. He watched the gap where it had been. He stood with his bare hand at his side, the dust gray along the side of his thumb, and the place on his palm where her hand had rested still keeping, absurdly, exactly, the shape of the pressure, until the sound of the wheels was gone under the sound of the river, and there was nothing left on the south drive but heat, and beeches, and himself.

That evening, he wrote to Mr. Gardiner. Or rather, he composed the letter four times in his head, walking the long way to it through room after room, and did not yet set pen to paper.

The freedom of the Pemberley water — that he could offer. That was a courtesy from one gentleman to another, requiring no lady's countenance and risking no lady's discomfort. Tackle to be sent down at whatever hour Mr. Gardiner named; a basket from the gardens, with Mrs. Reynolds's compliments to the ladies. All unexceptionable. All within the form.

He arranged it like a man setting locks on doors, sentence by sentence, each one defensible — and the absurdity, the thing he could have laughed at if it had been happening to any other man alive, was that he had opened the first door himself. He had ridden ahead to be at peace. He had arranged his solitude with care. And he had come round his own drive into the one face in England against which no locked door, no correct form, no careful little sentence could defend him.

He could not invite her. That much was fixed. He could not ask Mrs. Gardiner and her niece so much as to take a luncheon under his roof — the thing was impossible; Georgiana must come down on Monday and issue any invitation in her own person, by the form, as a sister and the lady of the house. He would observe the form though it cost him three days. On Monday the house would fill. On Tuesday he would call at the inn, with his sister, and conduct himself as a gentleman conducts himself toward the visiting relations of an acquaintance, and say what civility required and not one syllable beyond his command.

And after Tuesday he would not seek her out.

He arranged these resolutions in his mind in a neat defensive line, and stood at the window of the blue sitting-room with the untouched brandy at his elbow, and looked out at the sweep gone pale in the dusk and the south drive disappearing into its trees, and did not, for a long while, believe a single one of them.

The lamp behind him made the glass a poor mirror. His own face hung there, thin upon the darkening grounds, the line beside the mouth gone deeper than it had been in April. Beyond the pane the gravel sweep lost its heat by degrees, going gray under the first wash of evening; somewhere below the terrace the river kept up its low, indifferent running. Behind him, the clock in the passage struck the quarter with the subdued authority of a house that expected its master to be sensible.

He set his hand flat on the window-frame, on the warm polished oak — and felt, beneath his palm, impossibly, the ghost of that other pressure: light, exact, her gloved hand in his bare one, the whole of the afternoon concentrated into one square inch and refusing to fade.

He permitted himself, once, the word.

Hope.

Then he took it back, because he had no right to it, and a man who builds on a thing he has no right to is building on sand. She had no affection for him. He had her own word for that, given without softening, in a room he could not forget. The interval her hand had rested in his was precisely the interval good manners prescribed and nothing more; a lady cannot, without a public scene, refuse the hand that helps her to a carriage. It signified nothing.

It signified only that she had not drawn back.

It was nothing. It was less than nothing.

But it was more than he had had a month ago.

He stood at the window a long time, holding the small unauthorized warmth of it against everything he knew, and understood that whatever he had told himself about the estate, about the fences and the meadows and the stable wall, he was not, after all, going to be able to be at peace at Pemberley this summer.
